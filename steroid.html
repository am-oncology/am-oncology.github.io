Note on Steroid Withdrawal Syndrome (SWS): Fatigue, myalgia, nausea, mood disturbance, and joint pain may occur during taper even with normal adrenal function. SWS is a self-limiting neuroendocrine adjustment — it does not require reinstating steroids. Reassure the patient and avoid unnecessary cortisol testing.
Referral to Endocrinology should be reserved for patients who remain suppressed after 3–6 months on physiological replacement, or who have complicating clinical features. Dynamic testing (Synacthen) is not routinely required; morning cortisol is sufficient in most cases.
Dexamethasone → Prednisolone Conversion
Dexamethasone has a long half-life and does not cross-react with serum cortisol assays, making HPA axis testing unreliable. When assessment is needed, switch to prednisolone once dexamethasone is ≤0.5–1 mg/day, then continue tapering to 3 mg/day for cortisol testing.
Important: If the patient is clinically well and weaned to ≤0.5 mg/day dexamethasone, it is pragmatic to stop directly without switching or testing — provided no signs of adrenal insufficiency and no imminent physiological stress.

Dexamethasone — Key Clinical Considerations

Switching Protocol

  • Switch when dexamethasone ≤ 0.5–1 mg/day
  • Convert: 0.5 mg dex ≈ 2–3 mg prednisolone
  • Continue tapering prednisolone to 3 mg/day
  • Then perform 09:00 cortisol test ≥18h after last dose

If Unable to Switch

  • Wait ≥72 hours after last dexamethasone dose before testing
  • Cortisol may still appear low despite endogenous recovery
  • Interpret results with caution due to lingering dexamethasone effects
  • Consider Endocrinology input if unclear

CYP3A4 Interactions

  • Inhibitors (cobicistat, ritonavir, itraconazole, clarithromycin): increase steroid levels → risk of HPA suppression and Cushingoid features at low doses
  • Inducers (carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin): reduce steroid levels → taper more slowly; higher adrenal insufficiency risk
Conversion factors are approximate. Clinical judgement is required when switching preparations. If switching is not possible and HPA testing is still required, dexamethasone must be withheld for ≥72 hours — results must be interpreted with caution.

Sick Day Rules & Emergency Management
For patients who are stopping long-term glucocorticoids or who are at risk of adrenal insufficiency. Full guidance is available in guideline GUI0545.
Adrenal Crisis: If vomiting prevents oral steroid intake, or the patient is seriously unwell, they require immediate parenteral hydrocortisone (100 mg IM/IV) and emergency hospital attendance. This is a medical emergency.

Who needs sick day advice?

  • On ≥5 mg prednisolone/day (or equivalent) for ≥4 weeks
  • ≥3 high-dose oral steroid courses in the past year
  • Using multiple routes of glucocorticoid
  • Very potent topical steroids ≥200 g/week for ≥4 weeks
  • On CYP3A4 inhibitors with glucocorticoids

Dose doubling rule (minor illness)

  • Fever, significant infection, or physiological stress
  • Double (or increase to 5 mg twice daily) prednisolone for duration of illness
  • Hydrocortisone alternative: 10 mg three times daily
  • Return to usual dose when recovered
  • Do not reduce or stop steroids during illness

Symptoms of adrenal insufficiency

  • Profound fatigue and weakness
  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  • Dizziness, postural hypotension
  • Confusion or reduced consciousness
  • If in doubt: seek urgent medical review

Surgery & procedures

  • Inform anaesthetist and surgical team of steroid history
  • Minor procedures: double oral dose on the day
  • Major surgery: may require perioperative IV hydrocortisone (discuss with Endocrinology)
  • Do not stop steroids without endocrine advice pre-operatively

Steroid Emergency Card

  • Issue to all patients at risk of adrenal insufficiency
  • Card should be carried at all times
  • Instructs emergency staff on steroid treatment needed
  • Available from NHSE / Society for Endocrinology

When to refer to Endocrinology

  • Cortisol remains <150 nmol/L after 3–6 months on physiological replacement
  • Second suppressed cortisol result
  • Complicating clinical features or diagnostic uncertainty
  • Symptoms of adrenal insufficiency despite adequate replacement
Supportive Medications on Steroid Withdrawal

Adcal D3 (Calcium + Vitamin D)

  • If steroids were the only bone fragility risk factor: can stop once taper is complete
  • Continue if: older age, post-menopausal, prior fracture, low bone density, poor dietary intake
  • Formal bone health review (FRAX / QFracture / DEXA) once steroids stopped

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

  • If no other indication (reflux, peptic ulcer, NSAID use): withdraw after steroids stopped
  • To avoid rebound: step down gradually over 2–4 weeks, or alternate-day dosing
  • Mild reflux post-withdrawal is common and usually temporary
  • Reassess if symptoms persist or are severe
Sick day rules are for patients with known or suspected HPA axis suppression. Full guidance available in GUI0545. Patients should receive written information and verbal instruction; document that counselling has taken place in the clinical record.
